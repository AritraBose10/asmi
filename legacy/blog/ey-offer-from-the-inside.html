What a ₹20.38 LPA offer actually looks like from the inside

Arindam Dhali (MBA 2023–25) walks through his EY interview loop — the case rounds, the follow-ups, and the preparation that mattered most.

AD
Arindam Dhali MBA 2023–25 · Placed at EY
12 Mar 2026 8 min read
All posts
Written by Arindam Dhali MBA 2023–25 · Placed at EY
Published 12 Mar 2026 8 min read
Topic Placements

The number people ask about is ₹20.38 lakh. The number that actually mattered to me was four: the number of times I rehearsed the same twelve-minute story about a failed college project before anyone in a suit heard it.

I joined ASMi in 2023 with a commerce degree, no work experience and a vague belief that an MBA would sort the rest out. It did not sort anything out on its own. What it did was put me in a building where the preparation started early enough that, by the time the EY process began, the interview felt less like an examination and more like a conversation I had already had.

The loop, in order

The EY process ran over three weeks and four stages. There was a written aptitude round, a case discussion in a group of six, a technical interview that went deep on accounting fundamentals, and a partner-level conversation that was ostensibly about fit and was, in reality, about whether I could hold an opinion under mild pressure.

Nobody failed the aptitude round because it was hard. People failed it because they had not practised the specific pattern of question under a clock. Our placement cell had been running timed drills since the second semester, which by March had stopped feeling like preparation and started feeling like habit.

The group case is not about being right

Six candidates, one prompt about a retail client with declining margins in tier-two cities, twenty minutes. I watched two genuinely sharp candidates talk themselves out of the process by treating it as a debate to win.

The panel is not scoring your conclusion. They are scoring whether the group got somewhere better because you were in it.

What worked for me was small and unglamorous: I structured the problem out loud in the first ninety seconds, wrote the structure down, and then spent most of the remaining time asking other people to fill parts of it. The structure came from the case sessions in the marketing and operations core — not from a book on case cracking.

The technical round rewarded boring depth

This is where the classroom paid for itself. I was asked to walk through how a working-capital change flows through three statements, and then asked the same question again with a specific inventory write-down layered on top. There was no trick. There was just whether I actually understood the mechanics or had memorised a diagram.

Dr. Manta Dey's finance sessions had a habit of stopping mid-explanation and asking someone to continue. It is an uncomfortable teaching method and it is the reason I could keep talking when the interviewer went quiet.

What I would do differently

  • Start the internship conversation earlier. My summer internship shaped half of my interview answers, and I picked it late.
  • Keep a written log of every project, with numbers. I reconstructed mine from memory in week one of placement season and it showed.
  • Do fewer mock interviews with friends and more with people who will actually tell you that an answer was weak.

The part nobody puts in the brochure

Between the third and fourth round I was reasonably certain I had lost it. The technical interviewer had spent ten minutes on a question I fumbled, and I spent that evening in the student lounge convinced the process was over. It was not. The feedback afterwards noted that I had corrected myself unprompted, which apparently counted for more than getting it right first time.

If you are reading this while deciding whether to apply somewhere, the honest version is this: the offer is the visible part of a two-year process that is mostly invisible, mostly repetitive, and almost entirely about how early you start.
