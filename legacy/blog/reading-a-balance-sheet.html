Reading a balance sheet the way a recruiter wants you to

A practical walkthrough from the Finance Club's valuation bootcamp, with a worked Indian listed-company example.

MD
Dr. Manta Dey Associate Professor, Finance
20 Dec 2025 11 min read
All posts
Written by Dr. Manta Dey Associate Professor, Finance
Published 20 Dec 2025 11 min read
Topic Finance

Most students can define every line on a balance sheet and still cannot say what a company is doing. The gap between those two states is the entire point of the Finance Club's valuation bootcamp, and it closes faster than people expect.

Start with the shape, not the lines

Before reading a single number, look at proportions. What share of assets is fixed? How much of the balance sheet is funded by others' money? Is the current ratio comfortable or strained? Five seconds of shape-reading tells you what kind of business you are looking at, and every subsequent number either confirms or complicates that first read.

Three questions that do most of the work

  1. Where is the money tied up? Inventory, receivables and fixed assets each imply a different operating model and a different set of risks.
  2. Who funded it? The debt-equity mix tells you about risk appetite, cost of capital, and how much room management has if a bad year arrives.
  3. Is it getting better or worse? A single balance sheet is a photograph. Three years is a story, and the story is what recruiters ask about.

A worked example, in outline

Take a mid-cap Indian manufacturer. Receivables rising faster than revenue over three years is the signal to chase: either the company has extended credit terms to hold volume, or collections have deteriorated. Both are legitimate, and the difference matters enormously to valuation. Reading only the income statement, you would see growth. Reading the balance sheet alongside it, you see growth being bought.

Revenue tells you what a company sold. The balance sheet tells you what it gave up to sell it.

Where students consistently lose marks

  • Treating working capital as an afterthought rather than a driver of cash.
  • Quoting ratios without stating the comparison — a 2.1 current ratio means nothing without the peer or the prior year.
  • Ignoring the notes. The interesting liabilities are rarely on the face of the statement.
  • Describing rather than concluding. An interviewer wants a judgement, then the evidence.

How to practise

Pick one listed company in a sector you find genuinely interesting and read three consecutive annual reports. Write one page on what changed and why. Do this four times with four companies and you will interview better than candidates who have read twenty valuation frameworks.
