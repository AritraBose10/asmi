Why we made Business Analytics the spine of the BBA

The four-year BBA was rebuilt around analytics for a reason. A look at the labour-market data behind the decision.

SC
Soumyadeep Chakraborty Director & Chief Digital Officer
28 Feb 2026 6 min read
All posts
Written by Soumyadeep Chakraborty Director & Chief Digital Officer
Published 28 Feb 2026 6 min read
Topic Analytics

Most undergraduate business degrees teach management first and analytics later, usually as a final-year elective taught by whoever is available. We inverted that, and the reasoning was not pedagogical fashion. It was what happens to entry-level job descriptions.

What changed in the job description

Ten years ago, an entry-level business role asked for communication skills, a working knowledge of Excel and a willingness to learn. The same roles today ask for the ability to query a database, build a dashboard, and explain what the dashboard means to someone who will not open it themselves.

That third skill is the one that still separates candidates, and it is the one that only appears when analytical training and management training happen together rather than sequentially.

Why sequence matters more than content

If you teach marketing in year two and analytics in year four, students learn marketing as a set of narratives and then learn, too late, that the narratives are testable. If you teach the analytical toolkit first, every functional subject that follows can be taught as a measurable system.

A student who learns segmentation after learning clustering understands segmentation differently — and permanently.

So the BBA opens with spreadsheets, statistics and data fundamentals in year one, adds databases and visualisation in year two, and only then moves into predictive work and business intelligence in the specialisation year. The functional subjects are interleaved throughout, deliberately, so that each one arrives with a measurement lens already attached.

The IBM component

Curriculum and lab content across the analytics track are co-designed with IBM, and the wider Techno India Group's partnership with Google Cloud opens additional skilling pathways. What that means in practice for a student is unremarkable and important: the tooling in the lab resembles the tooling in the job.

The objection we hear most

Parents ask whether this makes the degree narrow — whether a student who wants marketing or HR is being pushed into a technical corner. The answer is the opposite. The analytics core is what lets a marketing student argue for a budget and an HR student defend a retention programme. It is general-purpose literacy, not a specialisation.

What we are still working on

Honesty requires noting the gap. Analytical fluency develops faster than the communication skills needed to use it, and we see students in year three who can build a model and cannot present it in five slides. The capstone and the club-led datathons are partly a corrective for that, and it is the part of the programme we revise most often.
